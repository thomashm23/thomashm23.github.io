About

I'm a climate scientist specializing in climate model evaluation and large-scale geospatial data analysis. My PhD research at the University of Arizona examines why models disagree about the current and future states of the stratospheric polar vortex and how much that disagreement limits confidence in their projections of related climate phenomena, such as the 2021 Texas cold air outbreak.

I build end-to-end Python pipelines (xarray, Dask, SciPy, Bash) for multi-model datasets, including CMIP6, CESM2 ensembles, and the ERA5, NCEP, MERRA-2, and JRA-55 reanalyses. My work centers on model validation, uncertainty quantification, and automated extreme event detection, with particular attention to how results depend on the reference dataset.

I'm finishing my PhD in May 2027 and am looking for roles in climate risk, energy, consulting, or applied research where climate data informs real decisions. Open to relocation or remote work.

Manuscript in preparation

Narrowing the gap in stratospheric polar vortex projections

Climate models can't agree on whether the winter polar vortex will strengthen or weaken, and that disagreement hasn't narrowed between model generations. Across 23 CMIP6 models, I show that most of the spread comes down to one physically grounded quantity, the stratosphere's equator-to-pole temperature gradient, which can be computed from standard model output.

Submitted · Geophysical Research Letters

Tracing a long-standing polar vortex bias in climate models

Many climate models simulate a winter polar vortex that's too strong. Comparing two versions of the same model, CESM2, against observations, I trace the bias to an exaggerated stratospheric temperature contrast in the version with a lower model top, pointing to model design rather than external forcing as the cause.

In revision

How fast are the tropics expanding? It depends on how you measure

The tropics are widening as the climate warms, shifting rainfall and desert regions, but estimates of how fast vary widely. Comparing seven ways of measuring the tropical edge across four reanalyses, I find that wind-based measures agree closely while one of the most widely used measures does not, and identify which to trust.

Methods project · 2024

Using spectral methods to decide which reanalysis to trust

When observational datasets disagree about a long-term trend, which one is closest to reality? I tested whether comparing each record's variability with the Arctic Oscillation can settle the question, and found ERA5 and JRA-55 the most consistent.
